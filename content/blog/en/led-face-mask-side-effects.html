Are LED face masks safe for your skin and your eyes, and can they cause cancer? The question comes up often, and it deserves a real answer rather than a reassuring “no risk at all”. Here is what the studies, the health authorities and the NŪR Protocol user manual say, and the few precautions that are enough to use an LED mask with peace of mind.

What the light of an LED mask does, and what it does not

An LED mask gives off visible light, red, blue, yellow, green, cyan, white or magenta depending on the setting. On NŪR Protocol, these lights range from 440 to 633 nanometres. This is not ultraviolet, the light of the sun and of tanning beds that causes sunburn and most sun-related skin cancers. Nor is it a laser. The light is diffuse, and a session lasts about ten minutes.

This explains the safety profile of LED masks in the literature. A systematic review of 31 randomised trials, published in 2018 in Lasers in Surgery and Medicine, found very few adverse events and concluded that dermatologists can use LEDs in their practice. The studies are often small, but none describes a serious danger in people who respect the contraindications.

The eyes, the precaution that matters

If one point deserves to be taken seriously, it is this one. In 2019, the French health agency ANSES confirmed that intense blue light is toxic to the retina, and that some people are more sensitive to it, in particular those with certain eye conditions or taking photosensitising medication. That same year, Neutrogena recalled its LED acne mask “out of an abundance of caution”, citing a theoretical risk to the eyes for a small group of users, with reported effects described as rare, mild and transient.

The answer is simple and not negotiable. You never look at the LEDs directly, and the two silicone eye protectors supplied with NŪR Protocol go in place before the mask is switched on. The user manual advises against the device for people with a hereditary eye condition. For any other eye condition, recent eye surgery or photosensitising treatment, ask an ophthalmologist before your first session.

LED masks and cancer

It is a common worry, and the answer is reassuring. LED masks do not emit ultraviolet light, and reviews of clinical studies report no link between their use and skin cancer. Common sense still applies. A mole that changes, or a spot that bleeds or does not heal, is a matter for a dermatologist, never for an LED mask.

Possible side effects

The most common is a slight redness just after the session. The NŪR Protocol user manual describes it as a normal reaction that fades within one to two hours. A burning sensation or any discomfort during the session, on the other hand, means stopping at once. Using the mask more often than planned brings nothing more, and the manual recommends three to five sessions a week, in four-week cycles.

Who should not use an LED mask

The NŪR Protocol user manual rules the device out during pregnancy and breastfeeding, for light-sensitive skin or photosensitising medication, and for people with a hereditary eye condition or a skin disease such as lupus, photosensitive eczema or albinism. NŪR Protocol is also intended for adults aged 18 and over. If you are taking a treatment and are not sure whether it makes your skin sensitive to light, your pharmacist can tell you in seconds.

Active skincare deserves a word too. Retinol, acids and some acne treatments make the skin more reactive. With NŪR Skincoach, these products are declared during the skin assessment so that your personalised routine accounts for them, and if a treatment was prescribed, the sequence is confirmed with your dermatologist.

The test to do before your first session

The user manual asks for a simple test. Place the device on your forearm and switch it on for ten minutes. If redness or itching appears, your skin may be sensitive to light, and you should stop and see a dermatologist before using it again. Every session then takes place on clean, dry skin without make-up, so that no light-sensitive ingredient in your cosmetics reacts to the light.

What dermatologists say

In the dermatology literature, LEDs are considered a safe and well-tolerated technique, which explains their success in clinics as well as at home. The most serious reviews remain cautious about the size of the results, for lack of large trials, but not about safety, as long as the eyes are protected and the contraindications respected. It is also why a serious brand publishes its specifications. NŪR Protocol lists its wavelengths, its 104 LEDs and its irradiance of 30 mW/cm² on its product page, and it is information you can ask any manufacturer for.

FAQ

Are LED face masks safe for the skin?

Yes, when used as intended. Studies report very few adverse events, most often a slight, passing redness. People concerned by the contraindications in the user manual should not use one.

Are LED face masks bad for your eyes?

Intense blue light can damage the retina, which is why you never look at the LEDs and wear the eye protectors supplied for the whole session. With an eye condition, an ophthalmologist’s advice is needed before use.

Can an LED face mask cause cancer?

LED masks do not emit ultraviolet light, and reviews of studies report no link with skin cancer. Any mark that changes should still be shown to a dermatologist.

Can I use an LED face mask while pregnant?

No. The NŪR Protocol user manual rules out pregnancy and breastfeeding, and NŪR Skincoach does not offer a routine during these periods.

Is it normal for my skin to be red after a session?

A slight redness that fades within one to two hours is described as normal in the user manual. A burning sensation during the session, however, means stopping at once.
